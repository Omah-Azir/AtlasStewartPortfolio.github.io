Research

Research moodboard

I pulled inventory and armory screens from dark fantasy RPGs. I added a ledger style menu from a medieval strategy game, a jewel matching puzzle game, Gothic cathedral stonework and a chart of medieval manuscript inks. Then I laid it all over a scan of an old woven wall hanging.

Plan

Style guide and palette

Every color in the UI comes off the moodboard. Warm greys handle structure. Earth tones handle surfaces and type. The bright jewel tones are saved for the jewels.

Plan

One frame for every screen

Before any art, I locked one frame for every screen. Player info sits in the top corners. Navigation sits in the bottom corners. The middle is for whatever that screen is about. So players never have to relearn where things are.

Problem

The problem I stepped into

The original menu was flat, static and hard to read. It was a blended camera over normal 2D UI, set in a graffiti font with no thought for legibility. I didn't build it. At the time I was a level designer and one of the producers.

The menu is the player's first look at the game. Ours gave them no reason to care about W-Dawg, or to feel like him. It made the whole game read as amateur before the first race.

We had no UI artists. Our two UI designers were stretched thin. The one on the menu was mostly doing production, and the other was handling the in-game UI. And the game was slipping out of focus. It was turning into a hodgepodge of people's personal projects and small-group projects.

  • Dropped two rolesI gave up producing and level design to take on the UI full time.
  • Stepped up as Creative DirectorI held everyone to the same standard so the game read as one experience again.
  • One style guideI worked closely with our art lead to lock a concise, final style guide.
  • Rebuilt from scratchI recreated the menu to fit the original design intent and style.

Plan

A menu inside the game world

I wanted the menu to feel like you're already in the game. So I borrowed from the Batman Arkham menus. The camera cycles through shots on its own. It pulls you in and shows you the characters before your first race.

  • Racers up frontI want players attached to the racers before the first race, so the menu is built around them.

Solution

Main menu in vector art

The look mixes vectordelia with Redline's cinematic style, on the white concrete of the menu world. The four options sit like a compass. Pick one to wake it up.

Prep Ride

W-Dawg's cyan · 00FFFF

A splash panel with high scores, a highlight clip, the game name and team name. Then Racer Select, a flat holographic overlay with the racer's face, info, track record and a bit of lore. Then two flash transitions, one into the track intro and one into the race start.

  • Plain names wonWe wanted every option to have an esoteric, in-world name. It cost more usability than it gave back, so the menu says Settings and Credits. Only play and the lore bible keep garage names: Prep Ride and Pit Crew.
  • Two plates per optionEach option has a resting plate and a lit plate. Hovering swaps them, so the halftone, tire tracks and arrows burst out around the word.
  • Colors from the castCyan is W-Dawg, pink is Lo Quei, yellow is Donnie and Momo. Credits get green, a color no racer owns, because you've stepped outside their world.

Quitting is themed too. Leaving the game brings up a "Racer Retiring" confirmation.

Build

How it's built in Unreal

I built the whole menu out of small, simple functions and data, so it scales. A new screen is a new row, not new code.

  • 17menu buttons
  • 8settings rows
  • 20small director functions
  • 69credit rows, built at runtime
  • Every screen is a rowOne Blueprint director holds every destination as a struct: its tag, its parent, its sequence and its buttons. Adding the three Garage hotspots took three rows and no new code.
  • One Back for everythingEach row knows its parent, so a single GoUp function handles Back on every screen, including loops and return sequences.
  • One input pathMouse raycasts, gamepad and keyboard all end in the same Interact call. One fix covers all three.
  • One interface, one materialEvery button and settings row talks to the director through one interface. Sliders are a single hologram material driven by one value. Credits and the Victory screen share one widget, fed by data tables.

Game feel deep dive · Not my game

Horns of Deliverance

A roguelike shooter where the chip in your head draws your HUD and wants you dead, and drugs are how you fight it. I played up to the boss and took notes out loud. This page follows one system, the drugs, from how the game shows them to what a dose does to your run.

My role

What I did

  • Played the early access build from first launch into the boss fight, and recorded it.See it →
  • Said my reactions out loud, then traced each drug from the HUD to what a dose does to the run.See it →
  • Turned each finding into a rule for my own game feel and HUD work.See it →

Horns of Deliverance belongs to its developer, schemesmith. I had no part in making it. Everything here is my read as a player, from my own recordings of one early access build.

Observe

Drugs against the chip

The chip draws your HUD, and it's counting down to your execution. Drugs are how you push back, and the chip's own HUD treats every dose like a crime.

  • The chip calls them contrabandYour drug inventory sits under a notice telling you to dispose of controlled substances right away. The same voice that counts your steps and sells you Premium is the one you're breaking.
  • A countdown sets the pressureThe HUD opens friendly, with good afternoon and no new notifications. Then it turns to one pending command, user execution, and a timer.
  • The chip fights backAs it loses its grip, its own panels scramble into smears of colored bars. The thing you're fighting breaks on screen.

Problem

Taking a dose

Press Q and your character lifts the item up to their face. You see the pill or the mushroom as a thing in your hand before you see it as a choice. That gives a dose weight. But the menu only shows up after the animation, about a second later.

That wait made me want to hold Q. Holding Q takes the item right away. So my instinct to wait for the menu became an accidental dose. To pick a different one I had to scroll and press F to confirm, all while shooting.

A wrong press changes what you see for the rest of the level. That makes this the one input that most needs to be safe.

  • Show the menu the moment Q goes down, and keep the lift as the animation behind it.
  • Let scrolling do the selecting. No confirm press in the middle of a fight.
  • Give taking a dose its own deliberate input, so waiting can never trigger it.

Observe

What a dose does

Each drug changes what you see and how you find the exit. The controls stay the same the whole time.

What it does to the run

  • More use, more healingEach dose heals you, and the heal grows the more you use.
  • A harder tripEvery reuse makes the trip heavier and raises the difficulty. The heal and the danger go up together.
  • ToleranceStay on one drug because you like how it plays and you'll slowly need more of it. So I kept switching.
  • Random pickupsYou can't plan around a favorite. You take what the run hands you, and each switch means new rules for reading the level.
  • Controls stay putEvery effect lands on sight and navigation. The thing you relearn is the level.
  • ThresholdsDelirants take a few bumps before the color drains. Too much of anything and you overdose.
  • MixingTwo pills at once give you a worse state with its own rules, and a maze for a level.
  • Wayfinding swapsOrbs, blood, a giant arrow, a maze. The exit stays the goal and the way you read it keeps changing.
  • A way to cut throughAiming down sights pulls the trip into focus for a second, right when you need to land a shot.
  • BlurVision blurs a lot, which made sniping hard. I'd rather the game pull focus. A focus shift feels like your body. A blur feels like a filter laid on top.

Analyze

Why it still holds together

Sometimes taking playability away expands an experience. This game goes further. It rug-pulls the game out from under you. Keeping the control scheme is the only way I can see to keep that cohesive. It's still the same game, with the same goals.

The extremes only work because the base game is simple. That's not an excuse. The game chose it. The core systems run on simple heuristics, so the player has cognitive load to spare when the screen falls apart.

And every effect makes sense in the story. Any game can take notes on building systems that are this narratively understandable. Even through obstacles that would normally be obtuse and frustrating, there's still a playable game underneath.

Analyze

How it gamifies drug use

There's no buff bar or timer for a high. You feel it through your eyes, while the numbers move underneath. It plays a lot like real use, written as rules.

  • The reward is healing that grows with every dose, and a push back against the chip.
  • The cost is a harder trip each time, which makes the whole game harder, and your read on the level.
  • Tolerance means comfort runs out. The drug you like stops being enough.
  • Random supply means you can't settle. You adapt to whatever you find.
  • The gamble is mixing, with the worst state in the game as the downside.
  • The framing never lets up. The HUD calls every dose contraband the whole time you depend on it.

The game also asks before any of this hits the screen. A Standard or No Visuals choice comes right after the content warnings.

Takeaway

What I'd take and what I'd leave

What I'd take into my own work:

  • States that change how you find your way while the controls stay the same.
  • Datamosh, pixelation and loss of focus as feedback for what a character is going through.
  • Mixing as a new state with its own rules.
  • A reward and a cost that rise together, so using more always costs something.
  • Tolerance that pushes players off their favorite playstyle.
  • A HUD voice that frames the mechanic, like the chip calling your drugs contraband.
  • Wayfinding from things in the world, like blood, before arrows.
  • A choice about heavy visual effects before the first run.

What I'd leave or change:

  • A delay between a button press and its menu.
  • Holding a button to use, when holding is also how a player waits.
  • A confirm press while the player is fighting.
  • Long sentences where a fighting player needs a glance.
  • Blur where a focus shift would say more.

Reflect

My story

  1. What went well

    Talking out loud while I played caught reactions I would have lost by writing afterward.

  2. What was tricky

    Telling taste apart from usability. The glass HUD is a taste call. The Q menu delay is a usability problem.

  3. How I worked through it

    I wrote each note the same way: what the game does, what I felt, and what I'd do. Then I checked my notes against the recording.

  4. What I'll carry forward

    How much I can get away with. Game feel can survive a lot, as long as it's designed for that from the very start.

Solution

Feather crosshair and HUD

In engine

Crow special VFX

The three crow-special Niagara systems in Unreal's preview, light to large. In the build, the larger levels spawned too small and the on-hit call broke, so these show the VFX as designed.

Plan

HUD feedback in four beats

I planned each HUD element as four beats. Something grabs your attention, something signals the change, the change happens, and it settles.

Health

Soul fluid in a container, top left

  1. AttractA splashy slash sound. Exposure jumps and a white vignette hits.
  2. SignalThe fluid flashes with extra glow.
  3. UpdateThe lost part greys out and wisps away like smoke.
  4. ResolveThe fluid settles, sloshing in a smaller space.

Enemy health

Drawn right on their body

  1. AttractThey grunt and react to the thunk of the arrow.
  2. SignalAn outline appears on them.
  3. UpdateA glowing white overlay fills them as they get "consumed by damage."
  4. ResolveBelow half, they switch to a damaged state.

Arrows

Flint heads, bottom left

  1. AttractThe bow drops as the arrow leaves with its trail.
  2. SignalOne glowing arrowhead leaves and the rest shift over.
  3. UpdateA reload "cathunk."
  4. ResolveThe bow comes back up.

Cooldown

The plumes on the crosshair

  1. AttractThe feathers glow for the level you picked and shake as the target gets marked.
  2. SignalWisps escape the glow.
  3. UpdateThe feathers go black and the glow drains into grey smoke.
  4. ResolveThe smoke clears and the glow slowly charges back up.

From Prof. Cagle: give it a signifier when it hits full power, like a sound or a flash.

Test

Audience testing

Before and during early production I ran qualitative sessions, closer to the audience testing and polling studios do before they commit to a game. I presented the pitch and early builds to artists, level designers, UX designers and people who'd never played Assassin's Creed or Far Cry, and polled how it felt. More playtests came later, once there was more to play. The aesthetic landed early. Most of the notes were about the HUD.

  • "It is very you. Which is good because that means you know how it should be."

Reflect

My story

Enhshahtén:ti is a tribute. It leans a lot on Assassin's Creed III and Connor, Ratonhnhaké:ton, from years of watching my older cousins play it. A little Severance went in too, in the idea that it's all in your head and they're testing you. The bow comes from Far Cry Primal, because its draw felt so good.

The feel comes from my own time outdoors. I've gone out alone or with the dogs and lived along a river for a few days. That feeling of knowing the land and moving on it like a predator is what I wanted to build, along with the quiet moments in stealth action games where you just take in the world.

01What went well

The core design held up. From the first pitch to the final build I barely had to iterate on it. The look I was chasing landed too. People called it dreamy, cold and ethereal, which is exactly where I was aiming.

All three levels of both attacks worked, each with its own custom sound, VFX and art. Damage reads on the enemy itself. As you hit them, their soul fills up and you take it. And watching a tester yell "He noticed me!" told me the enemy behavior was reading.

02What was tricky

Scope, and everyone knew it. Prof. Cagle's first notes on the pitch said it was overscoped, and he warned me the bow would take way more time than I thought. I pushed anyway because I loved the idea. I designed far past the brief: the story, the who and the why, the environment, the snow, the quiet moments. The end turned into a huge push and a crunch.

Some of it didn't make it.

  • The final UI animations never went in.
  • The crow VFX graded well but broke in the build.
  • The build crashed the morning of grading, and I had to fall back to my older submission.
  • The rest held up on its own, so I still got a good grade.

I also got ahead of myself. Every time I finished a system or a section, I gave it an art pass right away, before other areas even worked. I did that on purpose. There's psychology research suggesting that when a project looks good early, you like it more and want to keep working on it. It did keep me motivated. But it spent time the functionality needed.

03What I worked through

The brief was about feedback. The template we were given was hard to work with, though, so real feedback meant building the functionality too. It was practice for studio work, fitting into someone else's template and design patterns, even ones I didn't fully understand yet. I also cut the war hatchet I'd planned, since the brief didn't ask for it, and put that time into the three levels.

I sorted every test note into what I could act on and what was way too much for the time I had. The biggest change was the crosshair. The feather fan became the cooldown too, with one plume for each ability level, so the thing you aim with tells you what's ready.

With no team to hand things to, I had to pick my battles. Sound got priority, because a playtester was right. It can look rough as long as it sounds like it's tearing through the air. That showed in review, where the TA called out the audio work.

04What I'll carry forward

I don't have to carry all of it. Even on a simple project I can choose to simplify, and spend the early design time on what the brief needs and leave the world for later.

Next project, every system works before any of them gets pretty. Early art can still happen as one timeboxed pass.

And the UI goes in early, even if it's ugly. I spent this project planning to focus on UI and ended up doing it last. Next time the HUD works first, so testers react to the real thing.

Problem

The problem

Minecraft's UI isn't keeping up with how much happens in survival, or with how many blocks are in the game now.

Players run out of room. When you're building, you're limited to what fits. In combat, you can't keep every tool you need at hand. My rule going in was to add function and keep the simplicity players already like about it.

  • The Young ExplorerNewer to games, learned by watching older siblings, plays default survival. A small inventory and not much know-how can leave them overwhelmed. Sorting helps them find things, and backup equipment keeps them alive in longer fights.
  • The Hardcore SurvivalistPlays for hard feats and clutches, and leans on mods because vanilla doesn't help enough. They want less time sorting and digging for gear mid-fight, and a way to dump junk without a trip back to the lava at base.

Research

Research

I sorted about 50 posts by hand. It's a sample I coded myself, not a formal survey, but the themes were clear.

  • TerrariaClear sections for crafting, inventory and equipment, plus UI scale options. It gets cluttered with lots of items and runs small on consoles.
  • StarboundA second layer of hotbar slots gives more quick access. Fonts run small on TVs, and new players can find it a lot.
  • LEGO WorldsBig, friendly icons grouped by tool. Some icons have no labels, so players can't tell what they do.
  • Vintage StorySeveral inventory windows open at once, with drag and drop and stack splitting. Deep, with a steep learning curve and some scaling problems.

What I took from them: more quick-access slots, grouped tools and room to manage items, without the clutter or unlabeled icons.
