Case study · DES 360

Chain of Jewels

I built a style guide from a moodboard. Then I used it to design a full working UI for a dark fantasy jewel game. That meant a main menu, a shop, crafting and combat.

Role
UI/UX designer
Course
DES 360
Tools
Figma
Deliverables
Moodboard, palette, frame, 5 screens
MoodboardPaletteOne frameMain menuScreensNext

Step 1 · Research

It started as a wall of references

I pulled inventory and armory screens from dark fantasy RPGs. I added a ledger style menu from a medieval strategy game, a jewel matching puzzle game, Gothic cathedral stonework and a chart of medieval manuscript inks. Then I laid it all over a scan of an old woven wall hanging.

Step 2 · Style guide

A palette pulled straight from the board

Every color in the UI comes off the moodboard. Warm greys handle structure. Earth tones handle surfaces and type. The bright jewel tones are saved for the jewels.

Click a swatch to copy its hex code.

Step 3 · Greybox

One frame for every screen

Before any art, I locked one frame for every screen. Player info sits in the top corners. Navigation sits in the bottom corners. The middle is for whatever that screen is about. So players never have to relearn where things are.

Art passGreybox frame

Step 4 · Art pass

Reading the main menu

Tap the numbered pins to see why each part sits where it does.

  1. 01Who you arePortrait, level and XP stay pinned top left on every screen.
  2. 02What you haveEnergy and coins stay top right. So you always know what you can spend.
  3. 03One obvious next stepCampaign is the only big button, and it gets its own panel.
  4. 04Everything else, equalShops, Crafting, Book of Chains, Rewards and Quit share one row and one size.
  5. 05Out of the wayAudio and settings sit in the corners. Easy to reach and easy to ignore.

Step 5 · Screens

The same frame, different jobs

Reflection

What I'd do next

Two or three sentences in your voice: what playtests or critique told you, and what you would change in a second pass.

Case study · Shipping on Steam, December 2026

Nitro Guzzler

A Redline-inspired portal racing game. The main menu lives inside the game world. I'm Creative Director and UI/UX designer, and I created or directed all of the game's UI from concept to build.

Role
Creative Director, UI/UX
Team
31 · the Grease Monkeys
Engine
Unreal Engine 5.5
Release
Steam, Dec 2026
GoalWhite voidMenu mapGarageHow it's builtTrailerTeam

The goal

Make the menu part of the world

I wanted the menu to feel like you're already in the game. So I borrowed from the Batman Arkham menus. The camera cycles through shots on its own. It pulls you in and shows you the characters before your first race.

  • Racers up frontPlayers get invested in the racers, so I built the menu around them.
  • The menu is the attract loopA camera director hard cuts between sequences. The camera never blends. Only the stuff in the shot moves.
  • Names that fit the worldEvery destination gets a name from garage culture. Settings is the Garage. Credits is Monkey Business.

Intro

One white void for everything

The intro and the menu share one white space. There's no separate garage level. Cars race through it in their livery colors and leave colored tire stripes. Then they smash through a glass pane with the logo on it and switch to their in-game textures. The camera falls, and you see the team name written in tire tracks.

  1. 01IntroPlays once. Hold a button to skip.
  2. 02Ready loopWaits for the player.
  3. 03Any keyWakes the menu.
  4. 04Main menu loopThe attract loop takes over.
Intro capture goes here: livery-colored cars before the glass pane, in-game textures after

Menu map

A menu you read off the ground

The main options are arrows spray painted on the ground, laid out like a compass. Pick a direction to see where it goes.

W-Dawg

Play

Prep Ride

A splash panel with high scores, a highlight clip, the game name and team name. Then Racer Select. Then two flash transitions, one into the track intro and one into the race start.

Quitting is themed too. Leaving the game brings up a "Racer Retiring" confirmation.

Settings

Settings scratched into the paint

In the Garage, settings are hotspots right on W-Dawg's car. One Return button sits on the ground, so you can see it as the camera whips around. An artist on the team is making the etched decal art.

  • HoodVisualDisplay settings such as monitor size.
  • SideAccessibility + gameplayToggles like turning the HUD on or off.
  • Open trunkAudioVolume for each type of sound.

The other destinations work differently. They're flat holographic overlays you step through with Next and Return. Racer Select shows the racer's face, info, track record and a bit of lore, and Confirm locks them in. Pit Crew is a looping character sheet that cycles through every racer.

Before/after goes here: W-Dawg's bare hood vs. the hood with Visual settings etched in

Implementation

How it's built

  • One camera directorOne Blueprint holds every menu destination as a row of data. Each row stores where Back goes. So there's no special case logic for Back.
  • Structs over a Data TableData Tables fight with references to actors placed in the level, so destinations are an array of structs.
  • Hierarchy by tagEach destination has a parent tag. Garage's three hotspots are just three more rows tagged Garage.
  • Widgets on surfacesUMG widgets sit on 3D surfaces as Widget Components. They're physically separate but navigate as one screen.
  • One activation pathMouse clicks hit the UI through a raycast. Gamepad and keyboard step through an ordered list. Both call the same function.
  • Back plays in reverseEach destination sequence plays once and holds its last frame. Back plays it in reverse, reusing the game's rewind component.

I first planned a blended moving camera and a 2D widget stack. Once the real design was clear, I rewrote the plan around hard cut sequences and buttons on 3D surfaces. The Sequencer intro and the hand off into the attract loop are done in engine.

Trailer

The trailer

I based the trailer's structure on the Need for Speed Most Wanted trailer. Instead of showing off cars, ours follows W-Dawg drifting through our menus and intro. I wrote the shot lists and an artist made the shots.

Trailer goes here: W-Dawg drifts through Nitro Guzzler's main menu and intro

Leadership

Directing a 31-person team

I turned the menu and intro concept into a written game plan, a shot list and an Unreal Engine 5.5 implementation guide for the team. I direct artists from my shot lists and work closely with the design lead on team management.

Captures of the old and new menu, the UI's real colors and fonts, and any playtest feedback.

Case study · In progress

NOC-6000

The player is a robot. So the HUD is part of the robot. It's a hologram that floats out in the 3D world.

Focus
Diegetic HUD
Engine
Unreal Engine
References
Radar, aviation, green phosphor CRT
LayoutBehaviorBuild

Module layout

A HUD built from modules, like an OS

  • Sunrise moduleUpper left. Tracks the six moons as they rise.
  • Hunt bannerTop center. Time since the last hunt.
  • Clock + view directionUpper right. Mission clock and heading.
  • 3D crosshairCenter, with a floating distance readout.
  • Laser rangefinderCenter right. Ticks at 5, 10, 15, 25 and 50 m, intersecting the world like a construction laser.
  • Status + spearLower left and lower right.
HUD capture or sketch goes here

Behavior

A HUD that breathes

The crosshair widens after a miss and tightens when it's ready. It throbs a little, which makes the robot feel alive. Modules fade in when they matter and fade out when they don't. The hologram shifts between light and dark green.

Build

In engine

One master hologram material drives ring masks, radial ticks and layered opacity. A HUD rig Blueprint with four anchors is parented to the camera.

Case study · The site you're on

This portfolio

I built this site's colors from one red. Then I ran every color through color blindness simulations before I used any of them.

Role
Designer and builder
Tools
Coolors, ColorHexa, HTML
Focus
Color, contrast, accessibility
Five colorsTestingProcessOn this site

The system

Five colors, one job each

Every color here has a single job. That keeps the site easy to read, and it keeps me from guessing.

Click a swatch to copy its hex code.

Testing

Where red fails

I made the scheme in Coolors. Then I ran every color through ColorHexa's color blindness simulations and screenshotted the results. For a lot of people, the red falls apart. The turquoise holds up. So turquoise carries all the text here, and red only shows up as decoration.

Drag to compare my first pass with the final chart.

FinalFirst pass

Process

Showing my work

I didn't need a greybox for this one, since the palette itself was the job. I started by showing how close the darks were to each other. Then I added some rough flair so both halves of the chart were worth looking at. I wrote a note on every color so I could reuse the chart later. And I had some fun with the red failing. Last, I lined it all up with my style guide.

On this site

Where you can see it

  • TextAll of the text is the light turquoise. It reads on every background in the system.
  • GlowHeadings get a soft turquoise glow, the same one from the chart.
  • PanelsCards, notes and hovers sit on the darker teal.
  • RedThe red only shows up as a thread. It's in the scroll bar under the tabs and the edge of the open tab.
